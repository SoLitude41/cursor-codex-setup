# LinkedIn Organic Content Strategy for B2B SaaS — Playbook / SOP

Built from `research/` in this repository: 10 LinkedIn posts, 10 YouTube transcripts, and 10 supplementary sources (30 total) across Justin Welsh, Dave Gerhardt, Chris Walker, Ross Simmonds, Amanda Natividad, April Dunford, Peep Laja, Lenny Rachitsky, Kyle Poyar, and Gaetano Nino DiNardi. Full source links and collection dates are in `research/sources.md`; every claim below is cited individually against the specific source it came from.

Two sourcing notes that apply throughout, so I don't repeat them on every line: LinkedIn post dates are estimates decoded from the post's activity ID, accurate to about a day, not an exact timestamp — marked "(est.)" below. Two LinkedIn posts (Peep Laja, Lenny Rachitsky) and one YouTube video (Ross Simmonds) are flagged stale by the research index itself; I cite them where the content still holds up and flag it inline where it doesn't.

---

## Recommendations

### Foundation — before you write a single post

- **Positioning is upstream of content, not a byproduct of it.** Define what you're the best in the world at delivering, for a specific set of buyers, against a specific set of alternatives, before you decide what to post — messaging and content pillars both collapse without this input. (Source: April Dunford, "An Introduction to Positioning," https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning, 04.05.2020)

- **Match your first content motion to your actual growth mechanism**, not a generic template. Discovery-led businesses need SEO and comparison content; product-led businesses need onboarding and use-case content; sales-led businesses need the sales asset the team is already asking for. Figure out which one you are before picking a content type. (Source: Amanda Natividad, LinkedIn post, https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7472780094164467712/, 16.06.2026 est.)

- **Set two to three content pillars and hold the line on them.** They tell the audience (and the algorithm) what to expect, and they protect your own time and boundaries from scope creep into "share everything" territory. (Source: Amanda Natividad, YouTube interview with Corey Wilks, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kO5JXEwQ6LM, published 20.09.2025)

- **Go deep on one platform for a year or more before spreading to others.** Cross-posting efficiently only works once you've learned what actually resonates on a single platform; Justin Welsh spent three years LinkedIn-only before adding X, then Instagram and Threads a year and a half ago. (Source: Justin Welsh, YouTube interview, roompass channel, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0UxQbhaoMlQ, published 23.10.2025)

### Format and craft

- **Default to native, standalone content — video, carousels, text posts that deliver value without requiring a click.** The platform suppresses reach on outbound links, and dark-social referral tracking means you often can't measure the click anyway, so don't build your content plan around it. (Source: Amanda Natividad, YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kO5JXEwQ6LM, 20.09.2025)

- **Let the writing stay rough.** Typos, casual formatting, and sentences that don't sound machine-polished read as a costly, hard-to-fake signal that a real person is behind the post — and that signal is worth more as more LinkedIn content is visibly AI-written. (Source: Dave Gerhardt, "How I Post on LinkedIn," Exit Five newsletter, https://exitfive.com/newsletter/how-i-post-on-linkedin-now-that-i-have-200k-followers-exit-five-newsletter/, 18.11.2025; corroborated on his podcast — Dave Gerhardt, YouTube, The Dave Gerhardt Show, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qQ7wmDV153E, published 29.12.2025)

- **Open with the customer's story or the specific problem, not the stat or the feature list.** A named person's actual experience is harder to copy than an aggregated number, and it converts better than a display-ad-style claim. (Source: Gaetano Nino DiNardi, YouTube interview, Chili Piper channel, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2G79I9a7XIQ, published 02.12.2022)

- **State plainly who you're not a good fit for.** Naming your own limitations reads as more credible than an all-upside pitch, and it pre-qualifies the audience so sales doesn't waste calls on the wrong accounts. (Source: Gaetano Nino DiNardi, YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2G79I9a7XIQ, 02.12.2022)

- **Treat your long-form asset as the evidence file for your short-form claims, not a separate project.** Make the bold, compressed claim in the LinkedIn post; let the linked article or newsletter be where you prove it once someone wants the detail. (Source: Amanda Natividad, YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kO5JXEwQ6LM, 20.09.2025)

### Cadence and effort

- **Run "give" and "ask" as a ratio that shifts over time, not a fixed rule.** Start close to 90% pure value / 10% asks; only shift the ratio toward more asks as trust is actually earned, not on a schedule. (Source: Justin Welsh, YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0UxQbhaoMlQ, 23.10.2025)

- **Weight your plan toward consistent, modest posts over swinging for virality.** In newsletter growth — a reasonable proxy for owned-audience content generally — regular posts in the 50–150-subscriber range account for the majority of total growth, while the rare viral post is a small minority of it. Kyle Poyar reports the split as 55% from consistent "singles and doubles" versus 14% from top viral posts. (Source: Kyle Poyar, "How to grow your B2B newsletter," Growth Unhinged, https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/how-to-grow-your-b2b-newsletter, 11.02.2026)

### Distribution and amplification

- **Turn real-world moments into LinkedIn content instead of generating ideas from a blank page.** Announcements, stunts, even scrappy low-budget plays work as source material — the event happens once, the post amplifies it far past the room it happened in. (Source: Dave Gerhardt, YouTube, The Dave Gerhardt Show, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qQ7wmDV153E, 29.12.2025 — cites his own CEO-transition post: 1,426 likes, 218 comments, ~25,000 impressions on a Friday-morning post, historically his weakest posting slot)

- **Cross-promote with peers who have adjacent, non-competing audiences.** A guest post plus a mutual link-back borrows a second creator's trust rather than cold-starting your own — April Dunford's LinkedIn post about writing a guest essay for Lenny Rachitsky's newsletter, and linking to both the essay and his podcast, is a working example of the mechanic, not just a description of it. (Source: April Dunford, LinkedIn post, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/aprildunford_today-an-exec-says-to-me-must-be-easy-activity-7437914348683157504-VITK, 12.03.2026 est.)

### Monetization and measurement

- **Build up "algorithmic capital" with several value-only native posts before spending it.** Drop the ask — a webinar, a waitlist, a product mention — roughly once every four or five posts, not every post. (Source: Amanda Natividad, YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kO5JXEwQ6LM, 20.09.2025)

- **Don't treat an inbound demo request as automatically qualified.** A request for a live walkthrough before someone has engaged with any of your content is closer to a yellow flag than a hot lead — good content should be pre-answering the objections a demo exists to handle. (Source: Amanda Natividad, YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kO5JXEwQ6LM, 20.09.2025)

- **Expect six-plus months before brand-style content shows up as attributable pipeline, and don't kill a consistent effort at day 60 for lack of a direct line to revenue.** (Source: Peep Laja, YouTube interview, Databox channel, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F25QDJhbhgU, published 09.07.2025)

## Where Experts Disagree

### 1. How much polish a single post needs

- **Dave Gerhardt and Justin Welsh recommend** speed and volume over polish: post almost daily, write fast from a phone or notes app, leave the typos in, treat LinkedIn like a running blog rather than a publication. (Sources: Dave Gerhardt, Exit Five newsletter, 18.11.2025; Justin Welsh, YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0UxQbhaoMlQ, 23.10.2025)
- **Lenny Rachitsky recommends** the opposite for anything meant to carry real weight: 10 to 100 hours on a single piece, a dozen-plus re-reads hunting for "writing friction," a paid copy editor, cutting anything that doesn't earn its place — "kill your darlings," repeatedly. (Source: Lenny Rachitsky, YouTube interview, David Perell's *How I Write*, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DMZem1NYfpM, published 25.10.2023)
- **My take:** both are right for different jobs, and the mistake would be picking one rule for everything you publish. Native LinkedIn posts are a high-frequency, low-stakes format where the platform itself rewards a real, slightly rough voice over a polished one — Gerhardt's own reach data backs that up. But the asset those posts occasionally point to (a case study, a real guide, anything meant to be the "proof" behind a claim) should get Rachitsky's treatment, because that's the piece doing the actual persuading once someone clicks through. Applying Rachitsky's bar to every single post is a burnout plan; applying Gerhardt's bar to your one credibility-bearing asset per quarter is how you end up with nothing worth linking to.

### 2. Whether LinkedIn content should sell at all

- **Peep Laja recommends** avoiding what he calls LinkedIn "celebrities" — accounts posting generic, identity-less content built to rack up comments — and says that pattern doesn't work for an actual B2B brand, only for someone whose business model is attention itself. (Source: Peep Laja, YouTube interview, Databox channel, 09.07.2025)
- **Against that, most of the actual sampled posts in this research have a commercial angle woven in** — Justin Welsh's post sells a free guide that funnels into paid products, Kyle Poyar's and Lenny Rachitsky's posts carry an affiliate code and a sponsor mention respectively, Ross Simmonds' discloses a paid partnership. Purely value-only, zero-ask posts (Amanda Natividad's, Dave Gerhardt's reflective post) are the minority in this sample, not the norm.
- **My take:** Laja's actual target isn't "any post with a CTA" — it's content with no underlying point of view, optimized purely for engagement volume. Amanda Natividad's algorithmic-capital framing (mostly value, an ask roughly every fourth or fifth post) is the more precise rule and resolves the apparent conflict: the problem was never selling, it was selling with nothing behind it.

### 3. What LinkedIn is actually good for

- **Lenny Rachitsky recommends** treating LinkedIn as the stronger business channel outright — he reports it drives more traffic than X for him and calls it "shockingly powerful," specifically because it's business-oriented and allows longer posts. (Source: Lenny Rachitsky, YouTube interview, 25.10.2023)
- **April Dunford recommends** the opposite posture: she uses X, not LinkedIn, to stress-test half-formed ideas, because pushing a rough idea out on LinkedIn mostly gets reflexive agreement ("that's great, April!") while X actually surfaces disagreement and sharpens the thinking. (Source: April Dunford, YouTube interview, *Lenny's Podcast*, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hdjlCLb9Hl8, published 22.01.2023)
- **My take:** these aren't actually contradictory once you apply Amanda Natividad's own jobs-to-be-done lens to LinkedIn itself — Rachitsky is describing LinkedIn's job for *reach and business signal*, Dunford is describing its job for *idea development*, and both can be true about the same platform at once. The operational takeaway: use LinkedIn to publish and distribute conclusions you've already stress-tested somewhere with more friction, not as the place you go to find out if an idea is any good — the format's own incentive toward agreement will lie to you about that.

### 4. How narrow your niche should be

- **Amanda Natividad recommends** two to three fixed content pillars as hard guardrails, explicitly for focus and to prevent burnout. (Source: Amanda Natividad, YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kO5JXEwQ6LM, 20.09.2025)
- **Lenny Rachitsky explicitly rejects** the tighter version of this advice — what he calls "personal monopoly" thinking taken to its logical end (e.g., "I am the product management guy, full stop") — saying he'd be bored and unsustainable inside that box, and instead writes across a connected cluster (product, growth, careers, company-building) governed by an intuitive sense of fit rather than a fixed list. (Source: Lenny Rachitsky, YouTube interview, 25.10.2023)
- **My take:** side with Natividad for anyone earlier in the process. A fixed 2–3 pillar list is a training-wheels constraint that's easy to defend to a boss or a client and easy to keep yourself honest against. Rachitsky's looser, cluster-based approach is a late-stage privilege — it works for him because four years of consistent output already taught him where the edges of his own "intuitive fit" are. Starting a new account with his rule and no track record yet is just a way to justify posting about anything.

## What I Rejected and Why

- **Ross Simmonds' 2018 channel-spray playbook** (Quora answers, niche forums, Facebook groups, Slack communities, Medium as a distribution layer, paid LinkedIn ads, email-signature CTAs). Source: Ross Simmonds, YouTube, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wPEO-Gjld_E, published 11.03.2018. I rejected it for three reasons: it's eight years old and cites a 2016 referral-traffic stat as current evidence; it isn't actually about LinkedIn, it's general B2B lead-gen, and folding it in would turn a LinkedIn-specific playbook into a generic one; and it directly contradicts the more current, better-evidenced advice to go deep on one platform before spreading thin (see Justin Welsh, above). Keeping it would have padded the source count without adding a defensible recommendation.

- **Using Chris Walker's inner-work practice (daily journaling, belief reprogramming) as a prerequisite step before posting.** Source: Chris Walker, YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JkXom1dC_20, published 10.04.2025. He does connect an early bout of self-doubt to his own reluctance to post on LinkedIn, and the underlying point — that fear of judgment blocks people from publishing — is real. But turning his specific five-year journaling practice into a formal step in a content SOP would be importing a personal development regimen as if it were a repeatable input to content quality, on the strength of one person's correlation, not evidence that it generalizes. It doesn't belong in a document about what to post and when.

- **Peep Laja's full brand-tracking measurement system** (paid survey instrument, 100+ respondents, aided/unaided awareness tracked on a 6–12-month cadence). Source: Peep Laja, YouTube interview, 09.07.2025. The underlying principle — brand effects take months and won't show up in 30-day attribution — is in the Recommendations section above. The specific measurement infrastructure is not: it's built for a funded company running its own market-research function, not for a solo operator or small team whose whole question is "how do I post better." Including the full system would have been scope creep past what this playbook is actually for.

## My Original Ideas

### 1. A two-tier content ledger

None of the ten sources state this as a system, but it falls directly out of combining several of them: split every planned post into one of two tiers before you write it, and track the ratio between them.

- **Pulse posts** — native, written fast, rough on purpose, following the Gerhardt/Welsh model. These are the daily-or-near-daily volume.
- **Pillar posts** — rare (roughly monthly), built with Rachitsky's level of research and editing rigor, functioning as the "proof" asset Natividad describes — the thing a Pulse post can point to when a claim needs backing.

The reason this could work: Kyle Poyar's newsletter-growth data (55% of growth from consistent modest posts vs. 14% from viral hits) suggests the ratio matters more than either tier in isolation — a content plan built entirely from Pillar-tier effort is unsustainable at the cadence LinkedIn rewards, and one built entirely from Pulse-tier effort never produces the credibility asset that the Positioning-first recommendation (above) says you need. Naming the two tiers explicitly, and setting a deliberate ratio (e.g., roughly ten Pulse posts per Pillar post) turns a tension between four different experts' advice into an actual operating rule instead of a debate you have with yourself every time you sit down to write.

### 2. Write for the champion, not the ICP

April Dunford's positioning work defines "the champion" — the single internal person who has to build consensus and sell a purchase decision to everyone else in the company — as the only persona that matters in B2B sales, because if the champion doesn't get it, nothing downstream happens. (Source: April Dunford, YouTube interview, 22.01.2023) None of the ten sources apply this specifically to content targeting; they talk about ICPs, personas, and audience segments in the more conventional marketing sense.

The original idea: apply the champion framing to what you write on LinkedIn, not just to how you position the product in a sales conversation. Most B2B content is written for a vague "buyer" or a job-title-based persona. Writing instead for the specific person who will have to go argue for this internally — give them the language, the framing, the pre-built argument they can lift directly into a Slack message to their own boss — is a narrower, sharper target than "marketers at mid-market SaaS companies." It could work because it borrows a mechanism that's already proven to work in the sales conversation (Dunford's own case studies show champion-focused positioning closing deals that generic messaging doesn't) and applies it one step earlier, to the content that produces the champion in the first place.

## Weaknesses of This Playbook

- **One post, one video, one supplementary source per expert is not enough to see a pattern — only an example.** Real conclusions about what "works" for a given creator would need several posts per author sampled over time, not a single snapshot each. This playbook describes what ten specific pieces of content look like, and infers principles from them; it does not have the repeated-observation evidence to prove those principles are what each person does consistently.

- **Two of the ten LinkedIn posts are stale by the research index's own admission** (Peep Laja's is from November 2023, Lenny Rachitsky's is a September 2025 promotional/affiliate post rather than original advice content), and one YouTube source (Ross Simmonds) is from 2018. That's three of thirty sources — 10% of the corpus — that don't reflect current practice, and I only partially corrected for it by leaning on each expert's other, fresher sources instead.

- **Video selection wasn't vetted for topical relevance before collection, only for "this expert, a recent-ish video."** As a result, roughly a third of the YouTube sources (Chris Walker's almost entirely, Kyle Poyar's, and Ross Simmonds') contribute little or nothing specific to LinkedIn content strategy — Chris Walker's in particular is a mindset/journaling interview with only incidental mentions of marketing. That's a real gap in the underlying research, not just a citation-density issue.

- **Every expert in this sample is a working professional whose income partly depends on people believing organic content works.** There's no skeptic or buyer-side voice in the ten to stress-test the core premise that consistent LinkedIn posting is worth the time it costs a founder or small team. The playbook is built entirely from the producer side.

- **Heavy survivorship bias.** All ten people succeeded at this; none of them are examples of someone who posted consistently for a year and it didn't work. Some of what looks like a repeatable tactic (Lenny Rachitsky's Airbnb pedigree providing instant credibility, Dave Gerhardt's Drift background, April Dunford's published book) may be doing real work in their outcomes that a new, unknown account can't replicate by copying the posting behavior alone.

- **No independently verified performance data.** Every number in this playbook — Dave Gerhardt's 1,426 likes and 17 million yearly impressions, Kyle Poyar's 55%/14% growth split — is self-reported by the person it flatters. None of it is pulled from a neutral analytics source.

- **All ten sources are English-language and U.S.-centric in outlook, on a platform with a large non-U.S. B2B audience.** Nothing here has been checked against how LinkedIn content performs or reads in other markets.

## Who I Would NOT Recommend Following and Why

Of the ten, the one I'd flag is **Chris Walker** — with a specific caveat on what the flag actually means, because I don't think it's fair to extend the same logic to two other experts whose sampled sources also had problems.

Two of Chris Walker's three sourced pieces are genuinely strong and on-topic: his LinkedIn post (a sharp, well-built satirical take on Series A fundraising announcements) and his Passetto blog post on demand-generation content are both specific and credible. But the YouTube interview sourced for him — chosen because it's his own recent, current public content, the same selection method used for everyone else — turns out to be almost entirely about mindset work, journaling, "energetic levels," and personal transformation, with only glancing, secondhand references to marketing at all. That's not disqualifying by itself; plenty of credible operators also talk about mindset. What makes it a flag specifically for *this* purpose is that it doesn't read as an isolated appearance — based on how the interview is framed and how he describes his current business, it looks like a real and current share of what he now publishes. Someone going to learn LinkedIn or demand-gen content strategy from Chris Walker today has real odds of landing on content that isn't about that at all, and there are nine other people on this list who are more reliably on-topic for that specific job.

I'm deliberately not extending this same flag to Ross Simmonds or Peep Laja, even though their sampled sources were also weak (Ross's video is eight years old and off-topic; Peep's LinkedIn post is three years stale). In both of those cases, the other sourced material for the same person is current and strong, and the weak source looks like a collection problem on my end — not a pattern in what they actually publish. Treating "I picked a bad sample" and "this person's current output is unreliable" as the same thing would be an unearned swipe at two people whose other material clearly justified their place in this research.
